# What Larger Picture Is My Work Part Of?

When I started working at twenty-one, I did not really know what it meant to “do a good job.” I thought it was enough to arrive on time, carry out instructions, and be kind to customers. At my first part-time job, a fried chicken restaurant, I gradually began to see how the whole place worked. Once I understood how the dining area needed to respond when the kitchen fell behind, and how my actions connected with other people’s work, I began taking initiative without being told. At the time, I thought I was simply getting used to the job. Looking back, however, I was already learning something: understanding the bigger picture changed my attitude toward my own role.

My next job, loading and unloading parcels at night, was the opposite experience. I wanted to earn money, but I also thought, “If I can get through work this hard, perhaps I will be able to handle most things afterward.” Yet it was much harder than I expected from the very first shift. At first, dealing with people was harder than moving heavy objects. To experienced workers, moving parcels quickly, keeping the line running, and processing everything within the allotted time seemed entirely obvious.

To me, a newcomer, none of it was obvious. I did not understand why parcel handling had to move so fast, or how moving a single box a little too slowly affected the work that followed. I had not even properly learned which items belonged where. Yet the words I heard before any explanation were always much the same: “Hurry up.” Sometimes I was sworn at when I made a mistake.

What frustrated me most was not the scolding itself. I knew I was slow, but I did not understand why I had to be quite so fast. I kept thinking, “Why does this have to be done in such a hurry?” Nor was it an easy place to ask questions. Everyone was moving frantically, and as a newcomer, I felt that asking anything would interrupt the flow. Even asking about something I did not know made me anxious about how others would react.

Now I can understand the experienced workers’ position to some extent. They had enormous volumes to handle within a limited time, and parcels kept arriving while I was learning even one thing. After years of repetition, the work had become second nature to them; perhaps they could not understand why I could not do it. At the time, I did not realize that something completely obvious to an experienced worker might be anything but obvious to someone new.

As time passed, I began sharing meals with a few coworkers who treated me kindly. Through them, I started to hear how the loading and unloading operation fit together. “If you fall behind here, the next person falls behind.” “When parcels pile up here, the backlog keeps building further down the line.” “Ultimately, customers need to receive their packages on time, so we have to stay on schedule from this point onward.” Those explanations may sound unremarkable, but those few words meant a great deal to me.

Until then, all I had seen were boxes and conveyor belts. I thought it was a simple job: a box arrived, I moved it, and when another arrived, I moved that one too. After hearing those explanations, I began to see the people beyond my station. Someone took over the parcels I handled; after that came a delivery driver, and finally a customer waiting for a package. Only then did I understand that my delays made someone else’s job harder, and that delivering parcels on time was ultimately connected to customers’ trust in the company.

Strangely enough, from that point on, I began moving faster without anyone telling me to. Later, I was even assigned to demanding sections where large volumes had to be handled quickly. The work did not become physically easier. My mindset, however, was different. At first, “Hurry up” had felt like pressure. Once I understood the reason, that same urgency became a responsibility I accepted as my own.

Recently, while teaching grammar to elementary school students at an English academy, I had a similar experience. After explaining grammar to the children, I received feedback that I should not make the lessons too difficult. That was fair. Explaining everything I know to elementary school students does not make a good lesson. But on reflection, I realized that I did not sufficiently understand the level the academy was teaching toward or the educational direction it was pursuing.

I knew my task was to teach grammar, but I did not know where that task belonged within the overall curriculum. I wished I had learned a little more about the academy’s approach when I first took over the role. Had I known what the students had already learned, I would not have gone out of my way to introduce material from the next stage. I would also have found it much easier to judge where to stop an explanation. Both at the parcel facility and at the academy, I was not struggling because I wanted to do a poor job. I did not know what standards should guide my judgment.

These experiences made me feel that discussions of young workers’ rights need to consider more than legally guaranteed rights such as wages, rest breaks, and written employment contracts. We also need to consider the conditions that allow people to exercise those rights in practice and work in a healthy environment. Of course, legal rights are the essential foundation and must be upheld. Yet real workplaces also contain areas that the wording of the law alone cannot fully explain.

A few lines in an employment contract cannot teach someone what to prioritize in different situations, how far to exercise independent judgment, or when to ask questions. What we often call “good instincts” or “flexibility” at work seems to me less an innate ability than a capacity for judgment developed through understanding how an organization functions. The problem is that these standards feel so natural to experienced workers. It is easy to assume that what is obvious to us must also be obvious to someone who has just arrived.

At work, I sometimes hear comments such as “Young people these days have no sense of responsibility” or “It is because they are still young.” Among young workers, I also hear complaints such as “Why do they expect us to figure everything out as if it were obvious?” or “Older people are all so stuck in their ways.” Whenever I hear this, I feel that people are explaining one another through generational labels instead of trying to understand one another. Some young people certainly act irresponsibly, and to someone with years of experience, a newcomer’s behavior may look frustrating or careless. Still, looking back at the workplaces I have known, it is difficult to see this solely as a problem with one generation’s attitude.

In *Factfulness*, Hans Rosling describes the tendency to seek a simple explanation for complex problems by blaming a particular person or group as the “blame instinct.” Reading that passage made me think that recurring generational conflicts at work might follow a similar pattern. Saying “It is because they belong to the younger generation” or “It is because they belong to the older generation” makes a situation easy to explain. But once we reduce others to a single group, we may stop seeing why they came to hold different standards in the first place.

A young worker thinks, “How am I supposed to know when nobody has told me?” An experienced worker thinks, “Shouldn’t they be able to figure out at least this much?” Perhaps the two people are looking at different pictures in the same workplace. Especially for young people entering the labor market for the first time, what is missing may sometimes be a standard for judging responsibility, rather than responsibility itself.

I also wonder whether repeated experiences like these lead young people to take a step back from their work. Around me, I sometimes hear people say, “It is only a part-time job; this much should be enough,” or “I am still new, so I only need to do what I am told.” Of course, this does not explain every case. But if someone is repeatedly scolded without fully understanding what went wrong, feels uncomfortable asking questions, and cannot tell what doing well means even when they try hard, they may eventually choose to “just do what I am told” rather than exercise their own judgment. They may not have lacked motivation from the beginning. Their withdrawal may be the result of repeated frustration while trying to show initiative.

Self-determination theory in psychology suggests that people can develop more self-directed motivation when they experience autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Looking back at my parcel-handling job, that explanation feels familiar. When I did not understand why I had to move quickly, “Hurry up” felt only like control. Once I understood how my work connected to the next person and to the customer, I began to accept the same demand as my share of the work. What I needed was not relief from responsibility, but the context that would help me understand why that responsibility existed.

That is why I do not want to see young workers’ rights simply as a matter of protecting young people. An environment in which young workers can understand their labor, ask about what they do not know, and judge the boundaries of their responsibility is also an important condition for a healthy workplace. I am not arguing that responsibility should disappear. I am arguing that we should create the conditions that make it possible to take responsibility.

Employers need this too. One person’s repeated lateness, careless treatment of customers, or neglect of assigned duties does not affect that person alone. It burdens coworkers, affects the service customers experience, and can ultimately influence a business’s reputation and performance. Advocating for young workers’ rights cannot mean asking employers to understand and tolerate everything unconditionally. Instead, organizations should first explain clearly the responsibilities and attitudes they expect, and provide opportunities for the necessary training and feedback. If expectations still consistently fail to align afterward, both sides can reconsider how they work together.

What matters is not who puts up with more. It is sharing, from the outset, the picture that everyone needs to work toward. The approach I have in mind is not grand or complicated. Rather than handing a new worker only a list of tasks, explain what the organization does, what it provides to customers, where that person’s work fits into the process, and why it matters. Explain, too, which decisions they should make independently, when they should ask questions, and whom they should approach when a problem arises.

I have actually worked in that kind of environment. For a time, I worked in the kitchen of a busy gimbap restaurant for more than ten hours a day, five days a week. The owner did not simply say, “Make it this way” or “Work faster.” The owner often explained what kind of restaurant they wanted to build, how the food we sold was made, and what customers wanted in a neighborhood full of office workers. Little by little, I began to understand the picture the owner had in mind.

The work was certainly hard, but I developed affection for the restaurant and a sense of responsibility. I also felt proud that a part of the restaurant had been entrusted to me. I do not think the absence of major conflicts with coworkers was simply a matter of everyone being nice. To some extent, we shared the same picture of what we were working toward. It remains one of the jobs I remember most fondly, and it was also the first place where I felt that work could help a person grow.

This process is not only for young people. Newcomers can understand their roles more quickly, and experienced workers do not have to communicate what they consider “obvious” only through hints or scolding. Employers, too, can share their expectations of responsibility more clearly. Ultimately, it also reduces conflicts that cost everyone involved.

I will work in many organizations in the years ahead. Someday, I too will be in a position to teach someone how to do a job. If I find myself thinking, “Why can’t they figure out even this much?” I want to remember myself at twenty-one: someone who knew nothing but did not want to be lazy; someone who wanted to do well but did not know what doing well meant; someone who finally understood his share of the work, and wanted to do better on his own initiative, when another person explained the bigger picture in just a few words.

Across several workplaces, I have learned that a sense of responsibility does not emerge simply because it is demanded. When people understand what they need to do, why they need to do it, and where their work leads, they can begin to see their work as their own contribution rather than merely something they have been told to do. To me, young workers’ rights are therefore not about placing less responsibility on young people. They are about creating an environment in which young people can understand their work, ask questions, exercise judgment, and take responsibility for that judgment.

If people in the same workplace are looking at different pictures, what they need is not someone to blame. They need a way to see the same picture. Before asking young people to be responsible, we must first create an environment in which they can take responsibility. I believe that is where a healthy working environment for young people begins.
